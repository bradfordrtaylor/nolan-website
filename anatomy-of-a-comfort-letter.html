The Anatomy of a Comfort Letter

A Practitioner's Guide to the Most Misunderstood Deliverable in Securities Offerings

Harris Scott Co-Founder, Nolan AI March 2026

Introduction

If you work in capital markets, you have encountered comfort letters. If you are a junior associate at a law firm, you have almost certainly spent entire weekends preparing the backup that makes them possible. And if you are anyone else in the financial system (a banker, an investor, a compliance officer), you may have heard the term without ever fully understanding what goes into producing one.

That gap in understanding is not surprising. Comfort letters occupy a strange position in securities practice. They are not required by statute. They are never filed with the SEC. They do not appear in any public record. Yet they are a condition of virtually every underwriting agreement for registered securities offerings, and the process of preparing them consumes hundreds of associate hours per deal.

This article is a practitioner's walkthrough of the comfort letter process: what it is, who is involved, how the work actually gets done, and where the process breaks down. It is written for three audiences: junior associates who are about to experience this workflow for the first time, senior professionals who want to understand what their teams are actually doing, and anyone in the capital markets ecosystem who wants to know why this particular deliverable demands so much time and attention.

The comfort letter exists for one reason: to give underwriters documented evidence that they conducted a reasonable investigation of the financial information in a registration statement. Without it, they lack a critical component of their due diligence defense under Section 11 of the Securities Act of 1933.

What Is a Comfort Letter?

A comfort letter is a written statement from an issuer's independent auditors (typically a Big Four accounting firm) addressed to the underwriters and their legal counsel in connection with a securities offering. The letter confirms that the auditors have performed certain agreed-upon procedures on the financial information contained in the offering documents (the prospectus or registration statement) and reports the results.

The governing standard is AS 6101 (formerly AU Section 634), issued by the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB). Practitioners still commonly refer to the older AICPA standard, SAS 72, which AS 6101 replaced, and the term "SAS 72 letter" remains industry shorthand for the comfort letter itself.

What the Letter Covers

A typical comfort letter includes the following components:

  • Independence: A statement that the auditors are independent of the issuer within the meaning of the Securities Act and the applicable rules of the PCAOB.
  • Audited financials: Confirmation that the auditor has audited the issuer's annual financial statements and that those statements comply with the accounting requirements of the Securities Act.
  • Reviewed interim financials: Confirmation that the auditor has performed a review (not a full audit) of the issuer's most recent interim financial statements.
  • Subsequent period procedures: A description of procedures performed with respect to the period after the most recent balance sheet date included in the prospectus, typically covering changes in key financial line items.
  • Negative assurance: Negative assurance that nothing came to the auditor's attention indicating material changes in specified line items during the subsequent period (subject to the 135-day rule, discussed below).
  • Tick-and-tie: The results of "tick-and-tie" procedures: tracing financial information in the prospectus back to the issuer's accounting records and verifying arithmetical accuracy.

What It Does Not Cover

Comfort letters are limited to financial and accounting data. They do not cover narrative portions of the prospectus, such as risk factors, business descriptions, management discussion and analysis, or legal disclosures. They also do not cover information that cannot be traced to accounting records subject to the entity's internal controls. Under PCAOB standards, auditors may only provide comfort on information expressed in dollars (or percentages derived from dollar amounts) that originates from controlled accounting records.

Why the Comfort Letter Exists

The comfort letter is a creature of the due diligence defense. Section 11 of the Securities Act of 1933 imposes strict liability on certain parties (including underwriters) for material misstatements or omissions in a registration statement. The only way for an underwriter to avoid that liability is to demonstrate that it conducted a "reasonable investigation" and, after doing so, had "reasonable ground to believe" the registration statement was accurate.

For non-expertised portions of the registration statement (essentially everything other than the audited financial statements themselves), underwriters must show they performed their own independent investigation. The comfort letter is the documented evidence of that investigation with respect to financial data.

This is why underwriters insist on receiving comfort letters even though no statute requires them. The underwriting agreement almost always conditions closing on receipt of a satisfactory comfort letter. If the auditors cannot deliver one, or if the scope is inadequate, the deal does not close.

A comfort letter is not a guarantee that the financial information in the prospectus is accurate. It is documented evidence that specified procedures were performed and reported. The distinction matters: procedures short of an audit provide, at most, negative assurance, a statement that nothing came to the auditor's attention that caused concern. That is a materially lower standard than the positive assurance of a full audit.

Who Is Involved

The comfort letter process involves multiple parties, each with distinct roles and incentives. Understanding who does what, and where friction tends to arise, is essential to understanding why the process takes as long as it does.

Party Role in Comfort Letter Process
Company (Issuer) Provides financial statements, accounting records, and management support for amounts in the registration statement. Must be involved early because auditors will need source data that only the company can provide.
Company's Auditors Draft and deliver the comfort letter. Perform all procedures (tick-and-tie, subsequent period review, negative assurance). Negotiate scope with underwriters' counsel.
Underwriters Request and receive the comfort letter. Use it to establish their Section 11 due diligence defense. Control deal timeline.
Underwriters' Counsel Negotiate comfort letter scope and procedures with the auditors. Prepare the "circle-up", the marked document identifying every financial figure that requires verification. Drive the process day-to-day.
Issuer's Counsel Coordinates with underwriters' counsel on scope. Assists the company in preparing for auditor requests. Reviews the comfort letter for compliance.

The dynamic that shapes everything: underwriters' counsel determines the scope of the circle-up, the auditors perform the work, and the company provides the data. When any one of these three parties is slow or unprepared, the entire timeline slips.

The Circle-Up: Where the Real Work Begins

The circle-up is the most labor-intensive phase of the comfort letter process, and it is almost entirely manual.

Here is how it works. Underwriters' counsel receives the latest draft of the prospectus or registration statement, often a document running 100 to 300 pages for a complex offering. They then go through the document, page by page, and circle (or highlight) every financial figure that requires auditor verification. These figures appear both numerically and in written form, scattered across financial statements, footnotes, selected financial data tables, capitalization tables, dilution tables, MD&A, and the business description.

Every circled item becomes a line of work for the auditors. For each one, the auditors must:

  • Identify the source of the figure in the company's accounting records
  • Trace the figure back to audited or reviewed financial statements, or to records subject to the entity's internal controls
  • Verify that the figure in the prospectus matches the source
  • Confirm arithmetical accuracy where the figure is derived from other data
  • Document the procedure performed and the result (the "tick")

A single prospectus can contain hundreds of circled items. Each one requires a discrete verification step. And because prospectuses are revised multiple times during the course of an offering, often with changes to financial figures as the company updates its financials or responds to SEC comments, the circle-up is not a one-time exercise. It must be repeated or updated with every material revision.

This is where most of the associate hours go. A junior associate at underwriters' counsel or the issuer's law firm will spend days preparing and reconciling the backup documentation that supports the circle-up. When the prospectus is revised at 11 PM the night before pricing, the circle-up revisions happen overnight.

The 135-Day Rule

One of the most consequential technical details in the comfort letter process is the 135-day rule.

Under AS 6101, auditors may provide negative assurance (the statement that nothing came to their attention indicating material changes) only if 135 days or fewer have elapsed since the most recent audited or reviewed financial statements. If 135 days have passed, the auditor is limited to reporting the procedures performed and findings obtained, without the negative assurance language.

This matters because negative assurance is what underwriters want. It is the language their lawyers rely on to support the due diligence defense. Without it, the comfort letter is significantly less useful.

The 135-day rule creates real pressure on deal timing. Companies and underwriters must carefully plan offering schedules around fiscal quarter-end dates and the availability of reviewed interim financials. A deal that slips by a few weeks can cross the 135-day threshold and create material complications for the comfort letter, sometimes forcing the company to accelerate its quarterly review or restructure the offering timeline entirely.

Two Letters, Not One

Most practitioners refer to "the comfort letter" as if it were a single document. In practice, two are delivered:

The Initial Comfort Letter

Delivered on the pricing date (or the effective date of the registration statement). This is the comprehensive letter covering all procedures: independence, audited financials, reviewed interims, subsequent period procedures, negative assurance, and tick-and-tie results.

The Bring-Down Comfort Letter

Delivered on the closing date, typically two to three business days after pricing. This is a short-form letter that re-confirms the initial letter's conclusions as of a more recent date, specifically a date not more than three business days before the bring-down letter date. The bring-down does not repeat all procedures; it updates them.

The bring-down exists because things can change between pricing and closing. Underwriters need assurance that nothing material has shifted in the company's financial condition during that gap. If something has changed (an unexpected restatement, a material adverse event, a significant variance in a key line item), the bring-down letter will reflect it, and the deal may not close.

Where the Process Breaks Down

The comfort letter process is a known source of friction in securities offerings. The pain points are structural, not incidental. They arise from the nature of the work itself.

Manual Circle-Up at Scale

The circle-up is performed by hand on documents that can run hundreds of pages. Financial figures appear in tables, footnotes, narrative text, and exhibits. There is no systematic way to ensure every relevant figure has been identified, and no efficient method for updating circles when the document is revised. Associates perform this work manually, under time pressure, with the knowledge that a missed figure can create problems at closing.

Version Control Across Revisions

A typical offering involves multiple drafts of the prospectus. Each revision may change financial figures, sometimes in obvious places (the selected financial data table), sometimes in less obvious ones (a percentage buried in the risk factors section). Every change requires the circle-up to be updated, the auditors to re-perform their procedures, and the backup documentation to be reconciled. This cycle can repeat five, ten, or more times before pricing.

Data Tracing and Reconciliation

Auditors can only provide comfort on figures that trace to accounting records subject to the entity's internal controls. When the company's records are disorganized, when source documentation is difficult to locate, or when figures require complex derivations (currency translations, non-GAAP adjustments, pro forma calculations), the tracing process becomes significantly more time-consuming. Management support gaps (where the company cannot quickly provide the documentation the auditors need) are one of the most common sources of delay.

Late-Stage Scope Changes

Underwriters sometimes negotiate changes to the comfort letter scope during deal execution: adding procedures, expanding the list of circled items, or requesting coverage of additional financial data. These changes create additional work that must be completed within the existing deal timeline, compressing an already compressed process.

Coordination Overhead

The comfort letter requires real-time coordination among auditors, underwriters' counsel, issuer's counsel, and company management. Each party operates on its own schedule and priorities. Misalignment (a delayed response from management, a disagreement on scope between auditors and underwriters' counsel, a revision that arrives without advance notice) creates cascading delays.

Which Offerings Require Comfort Letters

Comfort letters are standard across virtually every type of securities offering where underwriters are involved:

  • Initial public offerings (IPOs): First-time public offerings with full registration statements filed with the SEC.
  • Follow-on equity offerings: Secondary offerings of common or preferred stock by already-public companies.
  • Debt offerings: Investment-grade and high-yield bond offerings, public debt issuances, and medium-term note programs.
  • Rule 144A offerings: Private placements to qualified institutional buyers under SEC Rule 144A. These require a signed representation letter from the financial intermediary and are often nearly as extensive as registered offerings.
  • Other transactions: Other structures involving underwriter participation, including tender offers, exchange offers, and consent solicitations.

The common thread is the presence of an underwriter (or initial purchaser) with a due diligence obligation. Wherever that obligation exists, a comfort letter is expected.

What This Means for Capital Markets Practice

The comfort letter process is one of the last major workflows in securities practice that remains almost entirely manual. Contract review has been partially automated. Document review has been partially automated. Due diligence questionnaires have been partially automated. But the circle-up, tick-and-tie, and backup preparation process has barely changed in decades.

This is not because the work is uniquely complex in a way that resists technology. It is because the work sits at the intersection of several challenging requirements: dense financial data, SEC-specific formatting and standards, cross-referencing across long documents, and the need for auditor sign-off under PCAOB standards. Until recently, the tools did not exist to handle that intersection reliably.

That is changing. Advances in AI, particularly in document understanding, structured data extraction, and financial reasoning, are creating the first real opportunity to meaningfully reduce the manual burden of comfort letter preparation. Not to eliminate human judgment from the process, but to automate the repetitive, verification-heavy steps that consume the most time.

For associates, that means fewer overnight sessions reconciling backup. For auditors, it means faster turnaround on tick-and-tie procedures. For underwriters, it means fewer delays attributable to comfort letter preparation. And for the capital markets ecosystem as a whole, it means a workflow that finally operates at the speed the rest of the deal does.
